# Two-Phase Commit (2PC) — YouTube Script
Duration: ~10 min | Target: Engineers 3–10 YOE | Episode: 49 of 93

## HOOK (0:00–0:30)
"Your payment service just debited a user's wallet by one hundred dollars. Then, right after, the order-creation call failed. The money is gone. There's no order. Now imagine the opposite bug — the order gets created, but the debit never happens. Free stuff, on the house, forever. Today I'm breaking down Two-Phase Commit — the protocol built to make two separate databases commit or roll back together — and exactly why, despite guaranteeing atomicity on paper, it can freeze your production database for five straight minutes if one process crashes at the wrong millisecond."

[Screen cue: Split screen — left side "Debit: SUCCESS", right side "Create Order: FAILED", with a red "$100 LOST" banner.]

## THE PROBLEM (0:30–2:00)
"Here's the setup. A user buys something. Step one: debit one hundred dollars from their account — that lives in Database A, the accounts DB. Step two: create an order record — that lives in Database B, a completely separate orders DB. Both must succeed, or both must fail. Within a single database this is trivial — BEGIN, UPDATE, INSERT, COMMIT, and either both changes happen or neither does, because one database engine controls the whole transaction. But the moment your update spans two separate databases, you lose that guarantee. Database A has no idea what's happening in Database B. You need an external protocol that coordinates both of them to commit or roll back as one unit. That protocol is Two-Phase Commit — 2PC for short. There's a coordinator, typically your order service, and there are participants, in this case the accounts DB and the orders DB. The coordinator's entire job is to make sure every participant agrees before anything becomes permanent."

[Screen cue: Draw the two-DB diagram — Order Service as coordinator, arrows to Accounts DB and Orders DB, both currently disconnected/uncoordinated.]

## THE SOLUTION (2:00–5:00)
"2PC runs in exactly two phases. Phase one is the PREPARE phase, also called the vote phase. The coordinator asks every participant, 'can you commit?' Each participant validates the change, locks the row it's about to modify, writes the change to its write-ahead log — the WAL — and replies YES or NO. After phase one, both databases are sitting in a PREPARED state: they've done all the work, they're holding locks, but nothing is permanent yet. Phase two is the COMMIT phase, the decision phase. If both participants said YES, the coordinator sends COMMIT to both, they commit for real and release their locks — transaction complete. If either participant said NO — say, disk full, or a constraint violation — the coordinator sends ABORT to both instead, both roll back their prepared transaction, release their locks, and you're left in a clean, untouched state. That's the happy path, and when everything works, it genuinely gives you atomicity across two independent databases. In practice this is usually implemented as an XA transaction — in Spring, you configure XA data sources for accounts and orders, wrap your service method in a plain at-Transactional annotation, and a JTA coordinator like Atomikos or Bitronix handles the two-phase protocol underneath for you. But here's the number that matters: a single-database transaction costs about two milliseconds. The same operation wrapped in XA 2PC costs ten to fifty milliseconds, because you're now paying for two full round trips to every participant plus coordinator overhead. At a thousand transactions per second, that's fine. At a hundred thousand transactions per second, 2PC becomes the bottleneck itself."

[Screen cue: Draw the two-phase timeline — PREPARE (both DBs lock + WAL + vote YES) → COMMIT (both DBs commit + release locks) — with a stopwatch showing 2ms vs 10-50ms.]

## DEEP DIVE — WHERE ENGINEERS GET IT WRONG (5:00–8:00)
"Here's the trap that makes 2PC dangerous in real systems: what happens when the coordinator itself crashes between phase one and phase two? Walk through the timeline. At time zero, the coordinator sends PREPARE to both databases. At five milliseconds, the accounts DB says YES — row locked, WAL written. At six milliseconds, the orders DB says YES too — row locked, WAL written. At seven milliseconds, the coordinator has received both YES votes and is about to send COMMIT... and right then, the coordinator crashes. Power failure, out-of-memory kill, network partition — doesn't matter which. Now both databases are sitting there, prepared, holding their locks, waiting for an instruction that never comes. They can't commit on their own — they don't know if the OTHER participant said yes. They can't roll back on their own either — they already voted yes, and the coordinator might come back and demand a commit. This is called the blocking problem of 2PC. Every other transaction that wants to touch those same rows is now blocked too — another user checking their account balance, another order for the same inventory item, all queued up behind locks nobody can release. If the coordinator takes five minutes to come back online, read its own write-ahead log, and finally send the COMMIT — that's five full minutes of lock contention on your production database. And the genuinely worst case: if the coordinator's own disk fails and its WAL is lost, there's no way to automatically recover the decision at all — someone has to manually inspect both databases and decide, by hand, whether to commit or roll back. This isn't a hypothetical footnote — it has happened at real companies running XA transactions in production. Beyond that one big failure mode, there's a whole matrix of them worth knowing for an interview: if a participant fails before voting, that's a clean abort. If a participant fails after voting YES but before receiving the coordinator's decision, that participant is blocked, holding locks, waiting. If the coordinator fails before sending PREPARE at all, nothing has started, so it's a clean abort too. If the coordinator fails while sending COMMIT — reaching database A but never reaching database B — you get a partial commit, the worst possible outcome, where the two databases have physically diverged. The fixes people reach for are three-phase commit, which adds a pre-commit phase and reduces but doesn't eliminate blocking; a coordinator built on Paxos or Raft so its state is replicated and survives a single crash; or — the answer most engineers actually reach for in interviews — avoiding 2PC entirely and using the Saga pattern instead. Here's exactly what that answer sounds like in an interview: instead of 2PC across the wallet service and the order service, do one single local transaction on the Orders DB that inserts the order as PENDING and inserts a row into an outbox table describing 'debit user wallet, this amount, this order ID' — that single-database transaction cannot have the coordinator-crash problem, because there's only one database and one WAL. A change-data-capture process like Debezium reads that outbox table and publishes the event onto Kafka. The Wallet Service consumes it, debits the wallet, and publishes back a WalletDebited event — or, if the user doesn't have the funds, a WalletDebitFailed event instead. The Order Service listens for WalletDebited and flips the order to CONFIRMED, or listens for WalletDebitFailed and flips it to CANCELLED — that CANCELLED flip is the Saga's compensating transaction, undoing the order instead of relying on a coordinator to undo two databases at once. No 2PC, no distributed locks, no coordinator to crash — just eventual consistency, on purpose."

## REAL WORLD (8:00–9:30)
"Picture a Paytm or PhonePe-style wallet debit paired with an order-service create-order call, running as classic XA 2PC across two databases — if the coordinator dies in that seven-millisecond window between receiving both YES votes and sending COMMIT, every wallet row involved is locked until the coordinator recovers, and at real transaction volumes that's not an acceptable few-minute freeze on people's money. Think about Swiggy or Zomato placing an order — that single 'place order' action really touches at least three services: create the order, reserve a delivery partner, and charge the customer's saved payment method. Running true 2PC across all three would work in theory, but at lunch-hour order volumes the ten-to-fifty-millisecond XA overhead per transaction, multiplied across every single order, would make checkout noticeably slower — which is exactly why food-delivery platforms lean on Saga instead, and only need 2PC as the mental model for understanding what Saga is compensating for. And imagine Flipkart during a flash sale: reserve inventory, process payment, confirm the order — three steps, potentially three services. 2PC across microservices at that scale is basically a non-starter; you'd be serializing every checkout behind coordinator round trips during your highest-traffic moment of the year. The honest answer in all three of these Indian-tech-scale examples is the same: 2PC is the correct textbook protocol for atomicity across two or three tightly-coupled databases, like core banking or enterprise ERP systems from SAP or Oracle that can tolerate the latency — but it is not what powers checkout at high-throughput consumer apps."

## OUTRO + NEXT EPISODE (9:30–10:00)
"So here's the takeaway: 2PC gives you real atomicity across databases — validate, lock, WAL, vote, then commit or abort — but it does it by holding locks hostage to a single coordinator's health, and if that coordinator dies between PREPARE and COMMIT, every participant is stuck until it recovers. Use it for two or three databases inside banking or enterprise systems where latency is acceptable. Avoid it in microservices and high-throughput systems — that's what Saga with compensating transactions is for.
