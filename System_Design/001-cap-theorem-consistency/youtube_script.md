## THE PROBLEM (0:30–2:00)
"Think about it this way — in a distributed system, you've got multiple servers, and sometimes the network between them breaks. That's called a partition. When that happens, you have to choose: do you keep responding to users, even if the data might be a little stale? Or do you refuse to respond until you're sure the data is correct? You literally cannot have both perfect correctness AND always-on availability at the same time during a network split. That's CAP theorem: Consistency, Availability, Partition tolerance — pick two. But here's the catch — partitions are NOT optional in the real world. Networks fail. So the real choice engineers make every day is: CP or AP."

[Screen cue: Draw a 3-circle Venn diagram labeled C / A / P, then cross out the "all three" overlap region with a red X.]

## THE SOLUTION (2:00–5:00)
"Now watch what happens when we apply this to real features. First: Amazon's shopping cart. During a network partition, Amazon chooses Availability — they'd rather show you a slightly out-of-date cart than an error page and a lost sale. If you added an item on one node and the network splits, someone reading from a different node might not see your latest item for a few seconds. And when the network heals, Amazon doesn't just pick a winner — they MERGE your cart using a CRDT, a conflict-free replicated data type, so items are never lost, just unioned together. Now flip to a bank transfer. Same network partition — but this time, if you go with Availability, you could deduct money from savings and NEVER credit checking. That's real money lost. So for financial transfers, you choose Consistency — you'd rather return an error and ask the user to retry than show a wrong balance. And third example: YouTube's view counter. At a billion views, you cannot have one atomic counter — every data center keeps its own count and merges every 30 seconds. Nobody cares if your view count is off by 35 views for a moment. That's Availability winning again, this time for a completely different reason — pure performance at scale."

[Screen cue: Draw three side-by-side boxes — "Shopping Cart → AP", "Bank Transfer → CP", "View Counter → AP" — green checkmark under each.]

## DEEP DIVE — WHERE ENGINEERS GET IT WRONG (5:00–8:00)
"Here's where I've seen even senior engineers get tripped up in interviews. The number one trap: saying 'I'll just use strong consistency everywhere to be safe.' Sounds responsible, right? It's actually wrong for three concrete reasons. One — performance: strong consistency means every write has to be confirmed by multiple replicas before it returns, so if you're doing 100,000 writes a second, you've just made every single one of them five times slower. Two — availability: if even ONE replica is down, strongly consistent writes get blocked entirely. During a routine rolling deployment, you'll get write errors. Three — it's often just unnecessary. Ask yourself: does it actually matter if a user sees their follower count as 1,247 instead of 1,248 for two seconds? No. Does it matter if their bank balance is wrong for two seconds? Absolutely. So the real skill isn't picking one consistency model for your whole system — it's picking the RIGHT consistency model per FEATURE. There's also a more useful mental model than CAP called PACELC — it says: during a Partition, choose Availability or Consistency, but Else, during completely normal operation, you're choosing between Latency and Consistency. That second choice — the 'Else' branch — is the one you're making every single day, because partitions are actually rare. And one more classic trap: read-your-own-writes. A user posts a status update, it's written to the primary database, then they immediately check their own profile — but that read gets routed to a replica that's 50 milliseconds behind. Their own post looks like it vanished. They panic and post it again — now you've got a duplicate. The fix is session-level routing: after a write, route that specific user's reads to the primary for the next few seconds, or use a read-after-write token tied to the database's log sequence number, like Aurora's Global Read Replica does."

## REAL WORLD (8:00–9:30)
"Let's ground this in companies you actually use. Think of a Flipkart-style e-commerce cart during Big Billion Days — that cart is designed AP, exactly like Amazon's, because losing a sale to five seconds of staleness is far worse than losing it to a 500 error. Now think of a PhonePe or Paytm money transfer — that's built CP. The system would rather show 'transaction failed, please retry' for two minutes than let two devices show conflicting account balances, because regulatory and financial correctness always wins over uptime in that flow. And for something like Hotstar's live view counter during an IPL final, when tens of millions of people are watching simultaneously, that counter is AP with approximate merging — nobody's cancelling their subscription because the live viewer count says 25.3 million instead of 25.31 million."
