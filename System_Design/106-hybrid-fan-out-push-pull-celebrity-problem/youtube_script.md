# Hybrid Fan-Out: Push vs Pull &amp; the Celebrity Problem — YouTube Script
Duration: ~10 min | Target: Engineers 3–10 YOE | Episode: 28 of 20 (Bonus)

## HOOK (0:00–0:30)
"A celebrity with 100 million followers hits 'post.' Fan it out the normal way, and even at a generous 500,000 writes per second across your entire fleet, that's 200 seconds just to finish ONE post — and if they post again 60 seconds later, your fan-out queue backs up further, degrading every OTHER user's feed too. Today I'm showing you the celebrity problem, and the hybrid push-pull architecture every major social platform quietly runs to survive it."

[Screen cue: A countdown "200 SECONDS" ticking while a fan-out queue visually backs up with other users' posts stuck behind it.]

## THE PROBLEM (0:30–2:00)
"Think about it this way — fan-out-on-write pushes a new post into every follower's precomputed feed the instant it's created, making reads cheap because the feed is already sitting there waiting. This works great for a normal user with 12,000 followers — that fan-out completes in under 100 milliseconds across your fleet. But apply the exact same strategy to a celebrity account with 100 million followers, and the math breaks: the write cost scales LINEARLY with follower count, so one post now requires 100 million individual feed-writes. Even with generous pipelined throughput, that's tens of seconds of fan-out work competing with every other user's post for the same shared write infrastructure — and it doesn't just slow down the celebrity's own post, it degrades fan-out latency for everyone else's posts too, a noisy-neighbor effect on shared infrastructure."

[Screen cue: Draw two fan-out jobs side by side — a normal user's 12,000-write job completing in 50ms vs a celebrity's 100-million-write job stretching across 42 seconds, with other jobs queued behind it.]

## THE SOLUTION (2:00–5:00)
"Now watch the hybrid fix: classify accounts by follower count, typically somewhere between 100,000 and 1 million as the threshold, and change the write path ONLY for accounts above it. A celebrity's post is written once to their own timeline table — an O(1) operation regardless of follower count — and the expensive 100-million-way fan-out job simply never runs. The cost moves to read time instead, but in a way that stays cheap: when ANY follower opens their feed, the read service does its normal precomputed-feed lookup for regular follows, exactly as before, and separately does a small number of EXTRA lookups — one per celebrity that specific user follows. Here's the key insight: while a celebrity might have 100 million followers, the number of celebrities any ONE user follows is small and bounded, usually single digits to low double digits, even for a power user. So you pull each celebrity's latest posts directly from their own timeline, merge-sort everything by timestamp with the precomputed feed, and return the page — total added latency is roughly 15 to 40 milliseconds, dominated by two or three small extra queries, not by how famous the celebrity is."

[Screen cue: Draw the read-time merge — a fast precomputed-feed read plus 2 small celebrity-timeline pulls, merge-sorted by timestamp into one final feed page.]

## DEEP DIVE — WHERE ENGINEERS GET IT WRONG (5:00–8:00)
"Here's the trap: picking the threshold wrong. Set it too low, and normal 'semi-influencer' accounts unnecessarily pay read-time complexity for every one of their followers. Set it too high, and upper-mid-tier accounts with hundreds of thousands of followers still saturate your write path before they cross the celebrity line. The real numbers make this concrete: at 100,000 followers, a fan-out completes in about 42 milliseconds — borderline but tolerable. At 1 million followers, that stretches to about 420 milliseconds — starting to matter under load. At 10 million followers, you're looking at 4.2 seconds — genuinely unacceptable write-path latency. This is exactly why 100K-to-1M is the typical cutoff zone, but the correct move is to VALIDATE it against your own fan-out fleet's measured throughput, not copy a number from another platform's blog post — monitor fan-out job completion latency per post and set the threshold where your p99 fan-out time crosses your own acceptable bound. Second trap: implementation detail that matters enormously — fan-out writes should NEVER be one round-trip per follower; use a pipelined batch write, like Redis pipelining for ZADD operations, which achieves 200,000 to 400,000 ops per second per shard instead of the vastly lower throughput of individual round-trips. And third: don't forget the merge step itself needs to be correct under concurrent posting — if a user follows three celebrities who all post within the same second, the read-time merge-sort has to correctly interleave all three by timestamp alongside the precomputed feed, not just append celebrity posts at the top regardless of actual recency."

[Screen cue: A table showing fan-out completion time climbing from 0.4ms at 1,000 followers to 42 seconds at 100 million, with a highlighted "typical cutoff zone" band between 100K and 1M.]

## REAL WORLD (8:00–9:30)
"Think about a platform with massive follower-count disparity, similar to X or Instagram at global scale — both have publicly discussed variants of exactly this push-pull hybrid, because without it, a single viral celebrity post would degrade feed delivery for every other user on the platform simultaneously. Think about Hotstar or a cricket-score platform during a match, where a small number of official commentary accounts have enormous follower counts compared to everyone else — the same threshold-based hybrid prevents official match-update posts from starving the fan-out infrastructure serving millions of ordinary users' regular posts. And think about any Indian social or content platform with a handful of mega-influencers alongside millions of regular creators — the exact same follower-count classification and read-time merge described here is what keeps both groups' content delivery fast simultaneously."
