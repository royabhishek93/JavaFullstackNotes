# Two-Level Ranking: Candidate Retrieval + Re-Ranking — YouTube Script
Duration: ~10 min | Target: Engineers 3–10 YOE | Episode: 29 of 20 (Bonus)

## HOOK (0:00–0:30)
"Your best machine learning model can predict exactly what a user will engage with. It also takes a millisecond per item to score. Run it over 2 million candidate posts, and you're looking at 2,000 seconds — thirty-three minutes — to load one feed. Today I'm showing you the two-stage funnel that every major platform uses to make that same precise model actually usable within a 150-millisecond budget."

[Screen cue: A stopwatch showing "33 MINUTES" crossed out in red, replaced by "150ms" in green.]

## THE PROBLEM (0:30–2:00)
"Think about it this way — you don't do a three-hour deep technical interview with every one of five thousand job applicants. You run a cheap resume screen first, narrowing five thousand down to fifty, and only THEN spend the expensive, high-signal interview time on those fifty. The screen doesn't need to be perfectly accurate — it just needs to not throw away people who would have been great hires. Feed ranking works identically: running your best, most expensive ranking model over the FULL candidate pool — millions of posts — is pure arithmetic failure. Even at a fraction of a millisecond per item, which is optimistic for a real deep-learning model, scoring millions of candidates takes seconds to minutes, completely outside any acceptable request latency budget of 100 to 200 milliseconds."

[Screen cue: Draw a resume pile of 5,000 shrinking to 50 via a cheap screen, then those 50 going through an expensive detailed interview.]

## THE SOLUTION (2:00–5:00)
"Now watch the actual two-stage funnel. Stage one is candidate retrieval — a cheap, fast, HIGH-RECALL pass whose only job is to shrink millions of candidates down to a few hundred, optimizing for 'don't accidentally drop good candidates,' even if some mediocre ones slip through too. This combines a simple recency filter — the most recent posts from people you follow — with an ANN, approximate nearest neighbor, search over content embeddings, using something like an HNSW graph index, which finds the 300 posts closest to your interest vector in latent space WITHOUT scanning all 2 million posts, resolving in just 2 to 10 milliseconds even over 10 million vectors. Stage one's whole budget is 20 to 50 milliseconds. Stage two is re-ranking — now that you're down to 300 to 500 candidates, the expensive, precise model can afford to score every single one, building a rich feature vector per candidate: recency, predicted engagement probability, an author-affinity score PRECOMPUTED OFFLINE from historical engagement, and content-type diversity signals. This stage optimizes for PRECISION, the opposite goal of stage one, and its budget is 50 to 100 milliseconds for batched inference over the shortlist. Total end-to-end: roughly 100 to 180 milliseconds, comfortably within budget, because the expensive model only ever sees a few hundred items, never millions."

[Screen cue: Draw the funnel — 2,000,000 candidates entering, shrinking through Stage 1 (ANN + recency, 20-50ms) to 300-500, then through Stage 2 (rich feature scoring, 50-100ms) down to the final top 20.]

## DEEP DIVE — WHERE ENGINEERS GET IT WRONG (5:00–8:00)
"Here's the trap that's invisible on a normal latency dashboard: stage-one recall quality silently degrading. If your candidate retrieval stage is too aggressive, or its embeddings go stale because the offline index hasn't been refreshed recently, you silently drop genuinely great content BEFORE the precise stage-two model ever gets a chance to see it — and no amount of tuning stage two fixes a problem that already happened one stage earlier. The mitigation is a specific offline metric: recall at K — continuously measuring, offline, what fraction of items that WOULD have ranked in the true top 20 under an exhaustive brute-force pass actually made it into the stage-one shortlist in the first place. This is the single most important health metric for a two-stage system, and it's completely invisible if you're only watching request latency, because a system with degraded recall can still respond in 100 milliseconds — it's just quietly serving worse recommendations. Second trap: treating stage-two's cost as free just because the candidate count is small. Batch inference cost still grows roughly linearly with candidate count, which is exactly why '300 to 500 candidates' is the practical ceiling for stage two, not an arbitrary round number — go much higher and you blow the 50-100 millisecond stage-two budget even with batching. Third: applying diversity constraints, like 'no more than 3 video posts in the top 10,' as a SEPARATE final pass after the raw model scores, not baked into the model itself — this keeps the precise ranking model focused purely on predicting engagement, while diversity rules stay easy to tune independently without retraining anything."

[Screen cue: A recall@K dashboard showing "Latency: 120ms ✓ GREEN" right next to "Recall@20: 62% ⚠ SILENTLY DEGRADED" — illustrating the invisible failure mode.]

## REAL WORLD (8:00–9:30)
"Think about Instagram's or a similar photo/video feed's ranking pipeline — precisely this two-stage funnel, recency plus embedding-similarity candidate retrieval feeding a learned engagement-prediction model, is publicly known architecture for how these platforms rank feeds within their latency SLAs. Think about a video platform like Hotstar surfacing 'what to watch next' during a live match break — candidate retrieval narrows down from the full catalog using content-similarity embeddings, and the precise re-ranking stage then weighs recency of the match, personal viewing history, and content-type diversity, all within the same tight latency budget users expect on a live-event page. And think about an e-commerce search-and-recommendation system at Flipkart or Myntra scale — 'products you might like' on a homepage uses exactly this same funnel: a cheap retrieval stage narrows millions of SKUs down to a few hundred relevant candidates, then a precise ranking model scores those few hundred using purchase-affinity and price-sensitivity features that would be far too expensive to compute across the entire catalog."
