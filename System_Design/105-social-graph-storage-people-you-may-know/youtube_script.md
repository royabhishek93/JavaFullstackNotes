## THE PROBLEM (0:30–2:00)
"Think about it this way — the naive design is one table, friendships with user_id and friend_id columns. 'Who does Alice follow' is a clean single query. But 'who follows Alice' — searching by friend_id — becomes a full scan or scatter query at 300 billion rows, because a table sharded by user_id puts Alice's OUTGOING connections on one shard, but everyone who follows her is scattered across whichever shard THEY happen to live on. This is the exact same filing-cabinet problem you'd hit if you only ever filed papers by sender, then someone asks you to find everything addressed TO a specific person."

[Screen cue: Draw one sharded table with Alice's outgoing edges on Shard 3, but her incoming edges scattered as tiny dots across 40 other shards.]

## THE SOLUTION (2:00–5:00)
"Now watch the fix: write every connection TWICE, deliberately denormalized. One row keyed by user_id, in a table called connections_by_user, for 'who does Alice know' — a single-partition read. A second row keyed by friend_id, in connections_by_friend, for 'who knows Alice' — also a single-partition read. Both writes happen together in one atomic logged batch in Cassandra, so they never drift apart. This costs 2x storage and 2x write operations per connection, but every single graph read becomes an O(1) partition lookup, running in 2 to 8 milliseconds regardless of how big the total graph grows to. Now for 'People You May Know' — this is a completely different problem, a graph algorithm, not a storage pattern. For each of Alice's first-degree connections, you fetch THEIR connections — that's your candidate pool of 'friends of friends.' You then count, for each candidate, how many distinct paths connect them to Alice — someone appearing via three different mutual friends ranks higher than someone appearing via just one. Filter out people Alice already knows and Alice herself, sort by mutual-connection count, and take the top 50."

[Screen cue: Draw the two-table bidirectional write, then a fan-out diagram — Alice's 500 friends, each contributing their own 500 friends, converging on a handful of high-mutual-count candidates.]

## DEEP DIVE — WHERE ENGINEERS GET IT WRONG (5:00–8:00)
"Here's the trap in the raw arithmetic: a single user with 500 connections, each of whom also has 500 connections, generates up to 250,000 raw candidate pairs BEFORE deduplication — for just ONE user's PYMK computation. Across a billion users, naive full fan-out generates 250 TRILLION intermediate pairs in a single batch pass, which blows out even a distributed Spark cluster's memory and shuffle I/O. The mitigations that make this tractable: cap the first-degree fan-out per user at their 200 most recent or most active connections rather than all 500 — bounding the worst case to 40,000 pairs per user instead of 250,000; for genuine super-connectors, like recruiters with 30,000 connections, randomly SAMPLE a few hundred of their connections for the fan-out, since the top candidates surface via multiple independent paths anyway, so mutual-count ranking stays statistically stable even under sampling; and use combiners — Spark's aggregateMessages pattern — to sum partial mutual-counts per-partition BEFORE the final shuffle, instead of materializing all 250,000 raw pairs and shuffling them whole. Second trap: never compute PYMK live on a page load. This is genuinely a nightly Spark GraphX batch job over the full graph snapshot, taking 4 to 6 hours across roughly 2,000 executor cores at LinkedIn-scale, with results written to a Redis cache — the online serving path is just a cache read under 10 milliseconds, versus minutes for a live 2-hop traversal. And a third distinction worth internalizing: if your product needs genuine multi-hop path-finding — 'shortest introduction path to a hiring manager' — Cassandra's partition-per-hop model explodes combinatorially, because each hop requires a fresh partition read; a native graph database like Neo4j, with index-free adjacency using in-memory pointer chasing instead of index lookups per hop, resolves a 3-to-4-hop shortest-path query in 10 to 80 milliseconds even across 50 million nodes."
