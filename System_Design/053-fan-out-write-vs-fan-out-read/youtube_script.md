# Fan-Out on Write vs Fan-Out on Read — YouTube Script
Duration: ~10 min | Target: Engineers 3–10 YOE | Episode: 53 of 93

## HOOK (0:00–0:30)
"Cristiano Ronaldo has fifty million followers on Instagram. He posts a photo. The naive answer is: push that post into all fifty million followers' feeds right now. Do that, and you've just queued fifty million Redis writes that take ten to thirty minutes to drain — and if fifty celebrities post at the same time, that's two and a half billion writes fighting for the same queue. Today I'm breaking down fan-out on write versus fan-out on read — the single decision that decides whether your social feed survives a celebrity post or falls over."

[Screen cue: A celebrity account icon with fifty million follower dots exploding outward, then a queue-depth graph spiking off the chart.]

## THE PROBLEM (0:30–2:00)
"Here's the actual question every feed system has to answer: when Cristiano posts, when exactly does each of his fifty million followers' feeds get updated? There are only two honest answers. Option one — push it immediately to everyone, so by the time a follower opens the app, their feed is already sitting there ready, computed in advance. Option two — don't touch anyone's feed at post time, and instead, the moment a follower opens their app, go fetch what everyone they follow has posted recently, merge it, and sort it live. Neither of those, done in isolation, actually works for a real social network. If you always push on write, a normal user with two hundred followers is totally fine — writing to two hundred feeds is instant. But a celebrity with fifty million followers means fifty million feed writes for a single post, and that queue can take ten to thirty minutes just to drain, so people open the app right after the post and simply don't see it yet — an inconsistency window baked into the architecture. If you always pull on read instead, posting is trivially cheap — one row insert into the Posts table, done. But now every single feed load has to go query every account you follow. A typical user follows somewhere between two hundred and five hundred accounts, so opening the feed means two hundred to five hundred database queries, merged and sorted on the fly. At one million users opening their feed at the same time, that's five hundred million database queries a second — completely impossible for any database to sustain."

[Screen cue: Split screen — left side "Fan-out on Write: push to all" with a fan of arrows hitting many inbox icons, right side "Fan-out on Read: pull at open" with arrows pulling from many source icons into one feed.]

## THE SOLUTION (2:00–5:00)
"So let's walk through both models properly, because each one is genuinely correct in its own zone. Fan-out on write, the push model: Cristiano posts, the Post Service inserts the post into the Posts DB and publishes a 'NewPost' event to Kafka, and gives him a 200 OK immediately — he doesn't wait for anything. Asynchronously, a fan-out worker picks up that Kafka event, fetches all fifty million of his follower IDs from the Followers table, and for every single one, does a Redis ZADD — feed colon follower_id, with the post's timestamp as the score and the post ID as the value — into that follower's personal Redis sorted set. When any follower opens the app afterward, reading their feed is just one command: ZRANGE feed colon their user ID, zero to nineteen, reversed, with scores — instant, under a millisecond, because the feed was already pre-built for them. That's the entire appeal of fan-out on write: reads are essentially free because all the expensive work happened once, at write time, in the background. Now flip it — fan-out on read, the pull model. Cristiano posts, and literally nothing else happens. One insert into the Posts DB. No fan-out worker, no Redis writes, nothing. The cost gets deferred entirely to read time. When a follower opens their feed, the Feed Service fetches their full following list — say five hundred accounts — and for every single one of those five hundred accounts, runs a query like 'select star from posts where user_id equals that account, order by created_at descending, limit ten.' Then it merges all those results across all five hundred accounts, sorts by timestamp, and returns the top twenty. Writing is now practically free — one insert — but reading got extremely expensive, because you're doing hundreds of database queries just to render one person's home feed."

[Screen cue: Two side-by-side flow diagrams — Fan-out on Write showing Kafka → fan-out worker → 50M ZADD calls → instant ZRANGE read; Fan-out on Read showing single insert → 500 SELECT queries at feed-open time → merge → sort.]

## DEEP DIVE — WHERE ENGINEERS GET IT WRONG (5:00–8:00)
"This is exactly where engineers who've only read about one of these two models fall into the trap, because the real, production answer that Instagram and every serious social network actually runs is a hybrid, and getting the hybrid wrong is what separates a junior design from a senior one. Here's how the hybrid actually works. On post: if the poster has fewer than ten thousand followers, do fan-out on write — push the post_id straight into all their followers' Redis feeds, because ten thousand writes takes about ten milliseconds, completely fine. But if the poster has ten thousand or more followers — flagged as a celebrity account in the Users table — skip fan-out entirely. Just insert the post into the Posts DB and stop there. On read: the follower's feed load does two things in parallel. Step one, pull the pre-built feed straight out of Redis with ZRANGE — this contains every post from regular accounts they follow, because those got fanned out on write already. Step two, for celebrities they follow — and a typical user only follows something like three to ten celebrities out of their five hundred total followings, not five hundred — run a direct query against the Posts DB for each celebrity's latest posts. That's three to ten queries, not five hundred. Then merge the pre-built Redis feed with those freshly-pulled celebrity posts, sort everything by timestamp, return the top twenty, and cache that merged result in Redis for about sixty seconds so you're not redoing the merge on every single refresh. Now, the traps engineers actually hit. Trap one: pure fan-out on write with no celebrity threshold at all — CR7 posts, fifty million Redis writes queue up, and it takes ten to thirty minutes to fully drain, so followers who open the app in that window simply don't see the post — an inconsistency window that looks like a bug but is actually architecturally guaranteed. Trap two, and this compounds trap one — fifty celebrities posting around the same time, say during a major sporting event, means fifty times fifty million, two and a half billion Redis writes competing for the same fan-out queue, and the entire fan-out pipeline backs up for everyone, not just celebrity followers. Trap three: pure fan-out on read with no pre-built cache at all — a user who follows a thousand accounts triggers a thousand database queries on every single feed refresh, and at scale, with a million users opening feeds simultaneously, you're asking the database for hundreds of millions of queries per second, which no database survives. Trap four, and this one is subtle: forgetting that fan-out on read needs its own indexing strategy — if your Posts table isn't partitioned or indexed by user_id plus created_at descending, that 'latest ten posts for this celebrity' query becomes a full table scan across potentially billions of rows, and your celebrity-pull path, which was supposed to be the cheap path in the hybrid, becomes the new bottleneck instead."

[Screen cue: Animated hybrid diagram — small accounts fanning out on write into Redis ZSETs, celebrity accounts skipping fan-out with an X mark, then on read: Redis ZRANGE merging with 3-10 direct celebrity queries into one sorted, cached feed.]

## REAL WORLD (8:00–9:30)
"Think about Swiggy or Zomato and how they'd handle a restaurant partner or food influencer account with a huge following posting a new menu update or offer — if every single follow got a synchronous push write the moment that account posted, a popular restaurant chain account with lakhs of followers would create the exact same write-storm problem CR7 creates on Instagram, which is why any feed-style feature at that scale needs the same small-account-versus-large-account split. Picture Flipkart's 'Following' style updates from big-brand seller accounts during a mega sale — a seller with millions of followers posting a flash-sale update needs the fan-out-on-read treatment, while a small individual seller with a handful of followers can safely be fanned out on write with zero risk. And imagine PhonePe or Paytm surfacing merchant offer updates in a personalized feed — a small local kirana store with three hundred followers gets pushed on write instantly, but a large national brand merchant with tens of millions of subscribers has to be pulled on read, or the offer-broadcast system would queue billions of writes exactly like the fifty-celebrities-posting-at-once scenario."
