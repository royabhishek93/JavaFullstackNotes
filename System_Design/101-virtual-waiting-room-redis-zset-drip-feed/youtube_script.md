## THE PROBLEM (0:30–2:00)
"Think about it this way — the instinct is to rate-limit: reject requests above some threshold. But that's fundamentally unfair. The 499,700th person to click isn't a malicious attacker, they're a genuine customer who deserves a ticket eventually, just not this exact millisecond. Rejecting them outright means they immediately retry, often instantly, which just worsens the storm — you get a retry stampede hammering you even harder than the original burst. A rate limiter also has no memory of who already tried, so a rejected user competes equally with brand-new arrivals on the very next second's wave, with no guarantee they ever get in at all."

[Screen cue: Draw a token-bucket rate limiter showing 500 passing through and 499,500 getting rejected with a 429 error, then those rejected users immediately retrying and hammering the system again.]

## THE SOLUTION (2:00–5:00)
"Now watch the better approach: give everyone a numbered ticket the instant they arrive, and process people strictly in arrival order, at a rate your backend can actually sustain. The moment a user hits 'join queue,' you don't let them anywhere near the booking flow — you just run one Redis command, `ZADD waiting_room <arrival_timestamp> <queueId>`. This is O(log N), so even 500,000 people joining in the same second is trivial for Redis, even though that exact burst would flatten a relational database. A background 'admission worker' runs on a fixed tick, say every 2 seconds, and pops the lowest-scored — meaning earliest-arrived — 2000 entries off the sorted set using `ZRANGE` and `ZREM`, admitting exactly that many people into the real booking flow with a short-lived token. Everyone else keeps polling their position with `ZRANK`, watching their number tick down, exactly like watching your position in a phone hold queue. This keeps the number of people ACTUALLY inside the expensive part of the system — where DB writes and seat locks happen — bounded to whatever your backend can safely handle, no matter how many hundreds of thousands are waiting."

[Screen cue: Draw the ZSET as a sorted line of ticket numbers, with an admission worker popping the front 2000 every 2 seconds and pushing them through a narrow gate into the booking flow.]

## DEEP DIVE — WHERE ENGINEERS GET IT WRONG (5:00–8:00)
"Here's the trap that silently shrinks your effective throughput: the abandoned admitted-slot problem. A user gets admitted into one of your precious 2000 concurrent booking slots, opens the tab, then walks away to make coffee. If nobody reclaims that slot, it sits idle for the FULL admitted-token TTL — say 5 minutes — while 428,390 people behind them are still waiting unnecessarily, and your effective admission rate silently degrades over the whole sale window. The fix is a reclaim strategy: either subscribe to Redis keyspace notifications for expired keys and decrement your active-bookers counter the moment an admitted token expires, or run a lightweight sweep job every 30 seconds that checks your active-bookers set against which admitted tokens have actually expired and removes the stale entries — trading a slightly slower ~30-second reclaim time for not depending on keyspace notification delivery guarantees, which are NOT reliably persisted or replicated across a Redis failover. Second consideration: the admission worker's `ZRANGE` then `ZREM` must itself be atomic — wrap it in a Lua script, or two worker replicas running in parallel could both read the same batch of 'oldest' entries and double-admit the same users. And a key architectural distinction worth internalizing: rate limiting rejects excess traffic with zero memory of who tried; a waiting room accepts everyone, remembers their exact position, and drains them fairly over time — for a scheduled burst like a ticket drop, that's the difference between a fair, calm eight-minute wait and a chaotic retry storm that never actually lets everyone through."

[Screen cue: A timeline showing an admitted slot sitting idle for 5 minutes while a queue counter behind it keeps climbing, then a reclaim sweep job firing and freeing the slot for the next person in line.]
