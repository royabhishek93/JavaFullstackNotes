## THE PROBLEM (0:30–2:00)
"Think about it this way — a bookstore clerk who's never spoken to you says 'you'll also love this,' not because they understand your taste, but because they noticed thousands of other customers who bought the book in your hand also bought that other book. That's collaborative filtering: reasoning about co-occurrence in behavior across a huge crowd, not about content. The underlying structure is a user-item interaction matrix — rows are users, columns are items, cells are preference signals like watch-time. At real scale, this matrix is enormous and almost entirely EMPTY, because any single user has only interacted with a tiny fraction of the catalog — that's why it's called sparse, and directly storing or filling in every blank cell is completely infeasible: 500 million users times 10 million items is 5 quadrillion cells."

[Screen cue: Draw the sparse user-item matrix with mostly blank cells and a handful of filled-in numbers scattered across it.]

## THE SOLUTION (2:00–5:00)
"Now watch the fix: matrix factorization. Instead of filling in every blank cell, you assume both users AND items can be described by a much smaller set of hidden 'latent factors' — maybe 100 numbers per user and per item — representing things like 'how much does this user like fast pacing,' even though nobody ever explicitly labeled that factor. The dot product of a user's 100-number vector and an item's 100-number vector approximates predicted preference. Instead of 5 quadrillion cells, you now store roughly 51 billion floats — about 204 gigabytes — tractable, and it captures the DOMINANT patterns hiding in that huge sparse matrix. Alternating Least Squares, ALS, is the standard distributed algorithm for computing these vectors, typically via Spark MLlib: fix the item vectors, solve for the best user vectors, then fix those and solve for the best item vectors, repeating for 10 to 20 iterations until it converges. And critically, platforms lean ITEM-based rather than user-based for the reusable similarity structure, because the item catalog — while large — is far more stable than the user population, which churns constantly with new signups and drifting tastes; an item-to-item similarity table can be precomputed once nightly and reused across millions of users."

[Screen cue: Draw the matrix factorization compression — the huge sparse matrix shrinking down into two much smaller user-factor and item-factor matrices, with a dot-product arrow producing a predicted score.]

## DEEP DIVE — WHERE ENGINEERS GET IT WRONG (5:00–8:00)
"Here's the trap: cold start. A brand-new user who signed up 30 seconds ago has an ENTIRELY blank row in the interaction matrix — matrix factorization has nothing to learn their latent vector from, so any dot product involving them is meaningless. Same problem in reverse for a brand-new video uploaded 10 minutes ago: zero co-occurrence signal exists yet for item-based similarity to compute from. Every production recommender needs a fallback layer for both cases — for new users, either ask a couple of onboarding questions to seed a rough vector from genre tags, or simply serve global popularity rankings until they accumulate five to ten real interactions; for new items, fall back to content-based signals like tags and descriptions, or push the new item to the CREATOR'S existing audience as a decent proxy signal even with zero direct interaction data yet. Second trap: architecture. You should NEVER run ALS training or full model inference on the live request path — the training job itself takes one to four hours on a distributed cluster. The correct split is offline batch training, nightly or hourly, that precomputes recommendation candidates and writes them to a fast key-value store, paired with an online serving path that's just a cache lookup plus a lightweight real-time re-ranking pass using cheap signals like time of day — deliberately NOT re-running the heavy model on every page load, which is what keeps total homepage recommendation latency well under 100 milliseconds even though the underlying computation is genuinely massive."

[Screen cue: Split diagram — a blank "new user" row with a red X over the dot-product formula; then the offline batch layer (hours, heavy) feeding into an online serving layer (milliseconds, cache-only) with a clear boundary line between them.]

## REAL WORLD (8:00–9:30)
"Think about Hotstar's 'Recommended for You' row during IPL season — item-based collaborative filtering, precomputed nightly, means a fresh viewer's homepage loads recommendations in single-digit milliseconds from a Redis cache, not from live model inference. Think about a music or video platform in India dealing with a constant stream of brand-new users during a marketing campaign — the cold-start fallback to popularity rankings and genre-based onboarding questions is exactly what keeps those new users engaged before the collaborative filtering model has enough behavioral data to kick in. And think about a platform like Flipkart's 'Customers who bought this also bought' feature on a product page — that's classic item-based collaborative filtering, precomputed once per catalog update and reused instantly across millions of product-page views without ever recomputing per request."
