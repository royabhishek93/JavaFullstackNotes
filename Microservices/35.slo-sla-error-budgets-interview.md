## Opening — When Interviewer Asks "How Do You Define Reliability Targets?"

---

"A lot of engineers say 'we aim for 99.9% uptime' without being able to answer what
that number is measured against, or what happens when they're about to miss it. The
SRE framework (from Google's SRE book) gives precise vocabulary for this: SLI, SLO,
SLA, and error budgets. Let me define each and show how they connect to actual
on-call decisions."

---

## Part 1 — The Vocabulary (in the right order — this trips people up)

---

```
SLI (Service Level Indicator) — the ACTUAL MEASUREMENT:
  "Percentage of requests that returned in under 200ms"
  "Percentage of requests that returned a 2xx/3xx status"
  This is a real, continuously measured number — the raw signal.

SLO (Service Level Objective) — the INTERNAL TARGET for that SLI:
  "99.9% of requests will return in under 200ms, measured over 30 days"
  This is what your team commits to internally, and what pages people
  when at risk.

SLA (Service Level Agreement) — the EXTERNAL, CONTRACTUAL promise:
  "If uptime falls below 99.5% this month, customer gets a service credit"
  This is USUALLY set looser than your internal SLO on purpose — you want
  breathing room to fix problems before you're contractually in breach.

RELATIONSHIP:  SLA (99.5%, external, financial penalty)
                  ⊂ SLO (99.9%, internal target, pages the team)
                  ⊂ SLI (the raw measurement, updated continuously)

  You always set SLO stricter than SLA — the gap is your safety margin.
  If your SLO breaches, you have time to fix things BEFORE the SLA
  (and its financial/contractual consequences) is at risk.
```

## Part 2 — Error Budget: The Concept That Changes How Teams Actually Work

---

"An error budget is just '100% minus your SLO', reframed as a spendable resource.
If your SLO is 99.9% availability over 30 days, your error budget is the remaining
0.1% — which is about 43 minutes of allowed downtime per month. The powerful part
isn't the math — it's what it lets you DECIDE."

```
ERROR BUDGET AS A DECISION TOOL:

  SLO = 99.9% over 30 days → error budget = 0.1% = ~43 minutes/month

  ┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
  │  Budget remaining: 30 minutes (used 13 of 43 this month)     │
  │                                                               │
  │  → Team can ship a risky feature, do a chaotic migration,    │
  │    take calculated risks — budget allows for some incidents  │
  └─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

  ┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
  │  Budget remaining: 0 minutes (already exhausted this month)  │
  │                                                               │
  │  → FEATURE FREEZE. All engineering effort shifts to           │
  │    reliability work until the budget resets next period.     │
  │    This isn't a punishment — it's a pre-agreed policy that    │
  │    removes the political fight over "can we ship this risky   │
  │    thing" by making it a number, not an opinion.               │
  └─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

"This is the real value: it turns 'should we prioritize reliability or new features
this sprint' from a subjective argument into a policy driven by a number everyone
already agreed to."

## Part 3 — Multi-Window, Multi-Burn-Rate Alerting (What Actually Pages Someone)

---

"A naive alert — 'page if SLO is breached' — is USELESS in practice, because by
the time a 30-day SLO is actually breached, the damage is already done and there's
nothing to react to. What you actually alert on is BURN RATE — how fast you're
consuming the error budget, so you catch problems while there's still budget left
to react."

```
BURN RATE = (actual error rate) / (error budget rate implied by the SLO)

  SLO = 99.9% → allowed error rate = 0.1%

  If your CURRENT error rate is 10% (100x the allowed 0.1%):
  burn_rate = 10% / 0.1% = 100x

  At 100x burn rate, you'd exhaust an ENTIRE MONTH's error budget in
  30 days / 100 = ~7.2 hours. That's exactly why alerting must be
  fast-reacting for high burn rates — a slow, 100x burn eats a month
  of budget in less than a day if unaddressed.
```

```
MULTI-WINDOW ALERTING (catches both fast AND slow burns):

  ┌────────────────┬──────────────┬─────────────────────────────────┐
  │ Burn Rate       │ Window        │ Action                          │
  ├────────────────┼──────────────┼─────────────────────────────────┤
  │ 14.4x            │ 1 hour        │ PAGE IMMEDIATELY                │
  │                  │               │ (exhausts budget in ~2 days)    │
  │ 6x               │ 6 hours       │ PAGE (business hours OK)        │
  │                  │               │ (exhausts budget in ~5 days)    │
  │ 1x                │ 3 days        │ TICKET, not page                │
  │                  │               │ (exactly on pace to use budget  │
  │                  │               │  exactly by period end)         │
  └────────────────┴──────────────┴─────────────────────────────────┘

  Using BOTH a short window (catch fast incidents quickly) and a long
  window (catch slow, chronic degradation that a short window's noise
  would otherwise mask) is what Google's SRE workbook recommends — a
  single-window alert either fires too late (long window) or is too
  noisy (short window alone, false-positives on brief blips)."
```

## Part 4 — Production Scenario

---

"At a fintech platform, our payment API SLO was 99.95% success rate over 28 days.
One release introduced a bug causing a 15% error rate for 20 minutes before rollback.
Under a naive 'alert if error rate > 1%' rule, that's exactly what fired — noisy,
imprecise, no sense of actual budget impact. After switching to multi-window burn
rate alerting: the 1-hour window burn rate hit ~150x within 3 minutes, paging on-call
immediately with a message like 'at this rate, you'll exhaust November's ENTIRE error
budget in 4 hours' — which gave on-call an immediate, quantified sense of urgency,
not just 'error rate is elevated, could be anything'."

## Interview Traps & Cross-Questions

---

```
Q: "Why not just aim for 100% uptime — isn't that always better?"
A: "100% is not just expensive, it's actively harmful to velocity — it implies
    zero tolerance for any deploy risk, which means no one ships anything.
    The error budget makes explicit that a NON-zero amount of unreliability
    is an accepted, budgeted cost of shipping features at a reasonable pace.
    99.999% (five nines) costs orders of magnitude more engineering effort
    than 99.9%, for most products that marginal reliability isn't worth it."

Q: "What's the difference between SLO and SLA numerically — should they be the same?"
A: "No — SLA should always be looser (e.g. SLA 99.5% vs SLO 99.9%). If they
    were equal, the moment you breach your internal target you're ALSO in
    contractual breach with no reaction time. The gap is your buffer to
    detect and fix before financial/contractual consequences hit."

Q: "How do you choose which SLI actually matters for a service?"
A: "I focus on what the USER experiences, not just what's easy to measure.
    'CPU utilization' is easy to measure but users don't feel it directly;
    'request latency at p99' and 'error rate' are what users actually
    experience. I pick SLIs from the user's perspective, not the
    infrastructure's."
```

## Quick Reference Card

```
TERM              DEFINITION                                  EXAMPLE
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
SLI                actual measured value                        "99.94% success rate this week"
SLO                internal target for the SLI                  "99.9% over 30 days"
SLA                external contractual promise (looser)         "99.5%, or service credit"
Error budget        100% - SLO, a spendable allowance             "43 min/month allowed downtime"
Burn rate            how fast the budget is being consumed         "14.4x = exhausts budget in ~2 days"
```
